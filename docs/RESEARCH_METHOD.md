# Research Method

## Purpose

Define how this project investigates competitive-position change between Shopee and Tokopedia in Indonesia without overstating evidence or starting from a predetermined causal story.

## Primary Research Question

How did Shopee's competitive position in Indonesia change relative to Tokopedia, and what observable market factors help explain that shift?

## Core Research Principle

Do **not** begin the project with a predetermined explanation for Shopee's rise or for any observed competitive shift.

Incorrect: “Shopee won because of X.”

Correct: “Investigate whether X is supported by available evidence.”

Potential explanations are hypotheses. Hypotheses may later be supported, contradicted, or left unresolved by the evidence.

## Evidence Hierarchy

Prefer higher-ranked evidence when sources conflict or when choosing what can support a claim:

1. Official company filings
2. Government / regulatory sources
3. Original research / industry reports
4. Reputable financial and news publications
5. Secondary aggregators
6. Search/social indicators

Lower-ranked sources can still be useful for context, triangulation, or hypothesis generation, but they do not automatically override higher-ranked evidence.

## Metric Distinctions

The following metrics are **not interchangeable**:

| Metric | Meaning (conceptual) | Must not be treated as |
|--------|----------------------|------------------------|
| GMV | Gross merchandise value / goods transacted (definition varies by source) | Revenue |
| Revenue | Recognized company revenue under a stated accounting definition | GMV or market share |
| Market share | Relative position on a defined base (often GMV or another stated base) | Search interest or traffic |
| Web traffic | Visits or related traffic estimates to web/app properties | GMV or revenue |
| Search interest | Relative search attention (e.g. Trends-style indices) | Market share |
| User activity | Engagement or activity metrics as defined by a source | Orders or GMV |
| Orders | Count of transactions/orders as defined by a source | GMV without average value context |
| AOV | Average order value (GMV- or sales-related average, definition-dependent) | Market share |

Analytical rule: always state the metric actually measured. Do not relabel one metric as another to fill a narrative gap.

## Analytical Rules

- Correlation does not establish causation.
- Search interest is not market share.
- Traffic is not GMV.
- GMV is not revenue.
- Third-party estimates must be labeled as estimates.
- Missing data must remain missing.
- Do not fabricate historical values.
- Preserve original data granularity.
- Competitive events require supporting sources.
- Conclusions must distinguish observation from interpretation.
- Conflicting sources must be documented.
- Uncertainty and limitations must be disclosed.
- Hypotheses must not be presented as findings without evidence.

## Analytical Layers

1. **Descriptive** — what relative indicators did over the study window
2. **Associative / relational** — which observable indicators moved alongside competitive changes
3. **Event context** — what documented events occurred near turning points
4. **Interpretive** — carefully labeled inferences, separate from measurements
5. **Forward-looking** — forecasts and scenarios with explicit assumptions (later gate)

## Claim Labeling Standard

Every material statement in analysis outputs should be identifiable as one of:

- **Fact** — directly supported by a cited source measurement or documented event
- **Estimate** — third-party or modeled value, labeled as such
- **Assumption** — explicit modeling or scenario premise
- **Inference** — interpretation beyond the raw measurement

## Conflict and Gap Handling

- If two sources disagree, document both and explain any chosen priority using the evidence hierarchy.
- If a desired metric is unavailable, leave it unavailable; do not invent a proxy series and present it as the missing metric.
- If a hypothesis lacks evidence, state that the evidence is insufficient.
